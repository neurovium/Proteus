# Proteus Schema Registry Meta-Model Guide
A companion to `proteus_meta_model.yaml`. 

## The problem this model solves
Imagine a research field — neurophysiology will be our running example (though other fields work just as well) — in which dozens of groups have each designed their own data schemas. One electrophysiology team records a column called `lfp`; another calls the exact same physical measurement `local_field_potential`; a third just says `voltage` and doesn't distinguish whether the data is raw broadband, low-pass filtered, or high-pass filtered multi-unit activity (just as in cosmology, one survey team might record `z_spec`, another `redshift_spectroscopic`, and a third simply `redshift`). Or consider action potential dynamics: one team records a physical measurement like `trough_to_peak_time`, another calls it `spike_width`, while a third abstracts it away entirely into a generic `putative_cell_type`, hiding the underlying measurement used to make that classification. Some teams annotated their schemas with references to formal vocabularies; most did not. Nobody coordinated, because nobody had to: each schema was built to serve one experiment, and it serves that experiment fine.

The trouble begins when you want to ask questions across experiments — to build a field-wide knowledge graph that grows as new data arrives. Now you need to know whether Team A's `lfp` and Team B's `voltage` are the same thing, related things, or false friends. The traditional answer is to convene a committee, define The Official Ontology, and ask everyone to adopt it. That approach has a long and well-populated graveyard, because it demands consensus before delivering value, and because it asks experimentalists to become ontologists.

This meta-model takes the opposite route. It is the blueprint for a registry: a system that accepts schemas exactly as they organically exist, records them faithfully, and then treats the correspondences between them as data — evidence-bearing, confidence-weighted, reviewable records that accumulate over time. Agreement is not a prerequisite of the system; it is an output.

A note on vocabulary before that describes a meta-model: a schema for describing schemas. The classes defined here (`RegistryClass`, `Mapping`, and so on) are not neurophysiology concepts — they are the shelving system of the library, not the books. The books are the registered schemas themselves.

## The shape of the model: three layers and a spine
Everything in the meta-model belongs to one of three layers, plus a provenance spine that runs through all of them.

The first layer is **bookkeeping**: which schemas exist in the world, who maintains them, and which frozen versions of them the registry has ingested. Two classes live here, `SourceSchema` and `SourceSchemaVersion`.

The second layer is **content**: the classes, properties, value sets, and internal relationships extracted from each registered schema. This is where `RegistryClass`, `RegistryProperty`, `ValueSet`, `SlotAssignment`, `Unit`, and `Relation` live. Everything in this layer is a faithful transcription of what some source schema says — the registry adds structure but never opinion.

The third layer is **connective tissue**: statements about how elements of different schemas relate to each other. This is the `Mapping` and `MappingSet` machinery, and it is where all the interesting epistemology lives — confidence scores, evidence, human review.

The spine is `ProvenanceInfo`: every object in the registry knows who created it, when, from what, and how.

The single most important design decision in the whole model is the wall between the second and third layers. The rest of this document builds up to why that wall exists.

## Principle one: identity by content
Every definitional object in the registry — every class, property, and value set — is identified by a `hash_id`: a cryptographic hash computed from the object's own content. If you have used git, you already understand this. A git commit's identity is its content; change one character and you have a different commit. The old one doesn't get overwritten — it remains exactly what it always was, and the new one points back to it.

The registry works the same way. When Team A registers a property named `lfp` (or `z_spec`) with the definition "low-pass filtered extracellular voltage" and value type `xsd:float`, that bundle of definitional content is hashed, and the hash becomes the property's permanent identity. If Team A later refines the definition, the result is not a modified property. It is a new property, with a new hash, whose provenance record says `derived_from: <the old hash>`. The old property persists untouched, and anything that referred to it still refers to exactly what it referred to before.

This is why the model has no version-number slot and — deliberately — no `modified_by` or `modified_at` slots. An immutable object cannot be modified; "modification" is the birth of a successor. Version history is not a number you increment but a chain you can walk: follow `derived_from` links backward and you have the full lineage of any concept, across arbitrarily many revisions, with every intermediate state still addressable.

The payoff for a registry is enormous. A mapping asserted against a specific hash can never silently drift in meaning, because the thing it points to can never change. When a schema is re-registered after an update, the elements that didn't change hash to the same identities — so the registry automatically knows which mappings still hold and which need revisiting. Deduplication is free: if two ingestion runs produce byte-identical definitions, they produce the same hash and are literally the same object.

One practical consequence: hashing only works if everyone serializes content the same way before hashing (same key order, same whitespace conventions, same expansion of abbreviated identifiers). The choice of canonicalization scheme is an implementation detail, but it must be made once, early, and never changed — changing it re-mints every identity in the registry.

## Principle two: what a thing is versus what we believe about it
Every fact in the registry is one of two kinds.

**Definitional facts** are what a source schema itself asserts. "`lfp` is a float, measured in microvolts, defined as the low-pass filtered extracellular voltage" — that is Team A speaking, transcribed. Definitional content lives inside entities and is exactly what gets hashed. If it changes, the entity's identity rightly changes, because the schema now says something different.

**Epistemic facts** are what someone — a curator, an algorithm — believes about how entities relate. "Team A's `lfp` is a close match to Team B's `voltage`, with confidence 0.91, proposed by an embedding model, not yet human-reviewed" (or "Team A's `z_spec` is a close match to Team B's `redshift`"). That is an opinion about the entities, held by a third party, subject to revision. Epistemic facts live in standalone `Mapping` records that point at entities from outside, and they are never part of any entity's hashed content.

Why is the wall load-bearing? Suppose mappings were stored inside entities, as a list attached to each property. Then discovering a new correspondence — a routine, everyday event in this system — would change the entity's content, hence its hash, hence its identity. Every other mapping pointing at the old hash would dangle. One new opinion would cascade into an identity crisis across the graph. Keeping opinions outside entities means the registry can accumulate, revise, and retract beliefs freely, at any rate, without ever disturbing what the beliefs are about.

The distinction also has a subtler face. The model includes a `Relation` class for directed links within the definitional layer — for instance, a source schema asserting that `Electrode` `isPartOf` `ProbeArray` (or `Detector` `isPartOf` `Spectrograph`). This looks superficially like a mapping, but it is not one: it is part of what the source schema says, so it belongs inside the definitional layer and participates in hashing. The test is always who is speaking. If the source schema is speaking, it is a Relation. If the registry's alignment process or a curator is speaking, it is a Mapping.

## Layer one: registering schemas
A `SourceSchema` is a schema as a social artifact: it has a title, a publisher, a contact, a homepage. It captures the fact that, say, the Neuropixels Open Data Schema exists as an ongoing project maintained by some collaboration.

A `SourceSchemaVersion` is one frozen snapshot of that schema, as actually ingested: which format it arrived in (a LinkML file, a JSON Schema, an NWB extension, a CSV data dictionary — the model expects heterogeneity and says so in its `SourceFormatEnum`), when it was issued, a checksum of the ingested artifact, and a pointer to the previous version. Every entity in the content layer carries a `defined_in` link to the exact version it was extracted from, so nothing in the registry ever floats free of its origin.

One boolean on `SourceSchema` deserves special mention: `is_hub`. The registry's strategy for connecting N schemas is not to map every schema to every other (which scales as N² and decays through chains of approximate matches) but to map each schema once to a small, deliberately minimal core vocabulary — the hub. The elegant move in this model is that the hub is not a separate mechanism. It is simply another registered schema, flagged `is_hub: true`. Its concepts are ordinary `RegistryClass` and `RegistryProperty` objects; mappings to it are ordinary `Mapping` records. The umbrella that unifies the field is built from the same bricks as everything under it.

## Layer two: what's inside a schema
A `RegistryClass` represents a concept or object type a schema talks about — an extracellular recording, an electrode contact, a sorted unit. A `RegistryProperty` represents an attribute — a spike width, a sampling rate, a cortical depth (or a redshift, an exposure time, a right ascension). Both inherit a common core from the abstract `RegistryEntity`: a preferred name, a required definition, optional aliases, and — importantly — a `source_native_id` preserving the verbatim identifier from the source, exactly as it appears in the wild (`lfp`, warts and all).

The insistence on these textual slots is not bureaucratic tidiness. Labels, aliases, verbatim field names, and above all definitions are the raw material that alignment algorithms feed on. A property with a rich definition can be matched by meaning; a property with only a cryptic name can be matched only by string similarity, which is where false friends thrive. The model makes `definition` required because every skipped definition is a future mapping that can't be found or can't be trusted.

Two design choices in this layer are worth understanding rather than just accepting.

First, properties are typed with structure, not strings. A `RegistryProperty`'s `value_type` must be either a standard datatype identifier (float, string, date...) or a reference to a `ValueSet` — a registered, enumerated list of permissible values (like putative cell types), each of which can carry its own definition and external meaning. And units are not free text but structured `Unit` objects that resolve to standard unit vocabularies (QUDT identifiers, UCUM codes). The reason is that in the physical sciences, typing is alignment evidence of unusual strength. If two candidate-matched properties are both floats in millivolts, that supports the match; if one is a float in millivolts and the other is a controlled vocabulary of strings, no amount of name similarity should save the match. Structured typing turns "the units are compatible" into something a machine can check — including compatible-after-conversion, as with milliseconds and seconds. Stringly-typed units would make this signal, and this veto, impossible.

Second, classes don't own properties; they borrow them, with local terms. A class is connected to its properties through `SlotAssignment` objects, each of which says: this class carries that property, and here, it is required, or repeatable, or bounded in cardinality, or subject to a constraint. This indirection matters because requiredness is not a fact about a property in isolation — the same `sampling_rate` (or `exposure_time`) property can be mandatory in one class and optional in another. Attaching constraints at the assignment lets one property definition be reused across many classes without duplication, which in turn means that when two classes share a property, the registry knows they share it — a structural signal the alignment layer will later exploit. Note that slot assignments are definitional (they are part of what the schema says about the class), so they participate in the class's hash: change a constraint, and the class is rightly a new class.

## Layer three: the mapping layer
A `Mapping` is a single record asserting a relationship between two entities. Its subject and object are identifiers — registry hashes for internal entities, or external vocabulary IRIs for outward links — which means one uniform record type covers all three situations that arise in practice: schema-to-schema, schema-to-hub, and schema-to-external-ontology.

The relationship itself, the predicate, comes from a small graded vocabulary rather than a single "same as" link, because sameness in real fields comes in shades. **Exact match** says the two are interchangeable for practical purposes. **Close match** says they are similar enough to interchange in some applications — `lfp` and broadband `voltage`, say: both voltages, different filtering processes, different noise structures (or spectroscopic and photometric redshift). **Broad and narrow match** capture hierarchy: `voltage` is broader than `lfp` (or `redshift` is broader than `z_spec`). **Related match** covers association without equivalence or hierarchy. One relation stands apart: strict **logical equivalence**, reserved for human-validated cases where downstream systems are explicitly licensed to reason with it. 

The grades are not decoration. Exact matches can be safely chained; close matches cannot — two hops through "pretty similar" can land you somewhere quite dissimilar — and the registry's query machinery respects that difference.

Around the core triple of subject–predicate–object, a mapping carries its epistemology. `mapping_justification` records how the mapping came to be, from a controlled list of processes: manual curation, lexical matching, embedding-similarity matching, structural matching, logical reasoning, or an ensemble of these. `confidence` scores it between 0 and 1. `mapping_tool` and `match_string` preserve the producing software and the actual evidence matched on. `author_id` and `reviewer_id` attribute it. In other words, a mapping is not a bare edge in a graph; it is a citable claim, with methods and evidence attached, the way a measurement in a paper is more than a number.

Then there is `review_status`, which gives every mapping a lifecycle. Mappings enter as **proposed** — typically machine-generated in bulk. A human reviewer, shown the claim and its evidence ("same units, embedding similarity 0.91, sibling properties also match"), promotes it to **accepted** or demotes it to **rejected**. When a schema evolves and a successor mapping replaces an old one, the old one becomes **superseded**. Two things about this lifecycle repay attention. Rejected mappings are kept, not deleted: a human judgment that two things are not the same is hard-won negative evidence, useful both for calibrating future matchers and for preventing the same false match from being re-proposed forever. And the lifecycle is what lets different consumers of the registry choose their own rigor: a conservative query traverses only accepted exact matches; an exploratory one includes proposed mappings above a confidence threshold. The registry doesn't decide how cautious to be — it records enough that every query can decide for itself.

Mappings are grouped into `MappingSet`s — one matcher run over one schema pair, or one curation campaign — each with its own description, provenance, and license. Beyond organizational hygiene, this grouping is what makes bulk operations sane: a bad matcher run can be superseded wholesale, and a mapping set can be exchanged with other projects as a self-contained, standard-format artifact. (For readers who know the ecosystem: the mapping layer deliberately mirrors the SSSOM community standard, slot for slot, so registry mappings interoperate with existing mapping tooling rather than living in a private format. The lifecycle status is the registry's one declared extension.)

## The provenance spine
Every definitional entity carries a `ProvenanceInfo` record: who or what created it, when, from which predecessors (`derived_from` — the lineage mechanism from Principle One), and by what method. Relations carry provenance too, and mappings carry their own attribution slots. The uniform rule is that nothing in the registry is anonymous: every object can answer "where did you come from?" This is what makes the registry trustworthy as shared infrastructure — and it is also what makes it repairable, because when an ingestion bug or a miscalibrated matcher is discovered, provenance is how you find everything it touched.

## The model in motion: four scenarios
1. **A new schema arrives.** A systems neuroscience collaboration (or the DarkSky survey) submits its recording catalog schema as a CSV data dictionary. The registry creates a `SourceSchema` (if new) and a `SourceSchemaVersion` with the artifact's checksum, then extracts content: each documented concept becomes a `RegistryClass`, each field a `RegistryProperty` with its verbatim name in `source_native_id`, its type and units structured, its prose description in `definition`. Everything is hashed, everything points to the version via `defined_in`. Nothing has been interpreted yet; the schema has merely been transcribed into common structure.
2. **Matching runs.** An alignment pipeline compares the new schema's properties against the hub. For the lab's `lfp` (or DarkSky's `z_spec`), it finds the hub concept for low-pass filtered local field potential (or spectroscopic redshift): names are similar, definitions are semantically close, both are dimensionless or identically unit-bound floats. It writes a `Mapping` — close match, embedding-based justification, confidence 0.93, evidence strings attached — into a new `MappingSet`, status proposed. Nothing about `lfp` itself changed; its hash is what it was.
3. **A human reviews.** A domain expert sees the proposed mapping with its evidence and, knowing the field, upgrades the judgment: this is an exact match. A curated mapping (manual justification, reviewer attributed, status accepted) now supersedes the machine's proposal. The expert never touched an ontology editor; they answered "is your `lfp` their local field potential?" (or "is your `z_spec` their spectroscopic redshift?") — the only question they were ever asked.
4. **The schema evolves.** The collaboration releases version 2, renaming a field and tightening a definition. Re-registration creates a new `SourceSchemaVersion`. Unchanged fields hash to their existing identities — their accepted mappings carry over untouched, for free. The changed field becomes a new entity, `derived_from` its predecessor; its old mappings are marked superseded, and only this one element re-enters the matching queue. The registry has grown by exactly the amount that the world changed.

## The philosophy, restated
Strip away the machinery and the model makes three commitments. Identity is content, so nothing ever shifts under your feet. Assertion is separated from belief, so opinions can accumulate and be revised without disturbing what they are opinions about. And agreement is data — graded, evidenced, attributed, reviewable — so the field's shared understanding can emerge from validated mappings instead of being legislated in advance. The registry asks nothing of experimentalists but their schemas and their yes/no/sort-of judgments; in exchange, every new experiment that registers arrives already connected, through the hub, to everything that came before.
